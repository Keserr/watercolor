Key Neutral Pairs

Ultramarine + Burnt Sienna

Primary neutral pair. Granulates at all ratios. Near-equal = neutral chromatic gray. Blue-dominant = cool shadow. Sienna-dominant = warm earth.

Cobalt Blue Light + Yellow Ochre

Produces muted naturalistic greens and stone neutrals. Gentler granulation. Excellent for aged plaster, limestone, and distant foliage.

Ultramarine + Burnt Umber

Cooler and darker than Ultramarine/Sienna. Deep architectural shadows in overcast light. Neutral sits in a blue-gray zone.

Quin Rose + Ultramarine

Violet range for shadow color. From warm pink-violet to deep blue-violet. Essential for evening light and reflected sky.

Cross-Palette Mixing

When to reach beyond the primary 18 — and specific cross-palette pairs by function.

Cross-Palette Priority Rules

Default: Primary 18 Only

Start here always. Mix all greens, grays, and violets from within the primary palette before reaching for any secondary or tertiary color.

Reach for Secondary 24 when...

The color is genuinely unmixable from primaries (e.g., strong Cerulean sky, deep Indigo night, Carbazole Violet), or the secondary color saves significant mixing time without compromising the painting.

Reach for Tertiary 14 when...

The subject calls for granulation that the primary palette can't provide — loose skies, water surfaces, stone texture, or experimental atmospheric work.

Never mix Phthalo (PG7/PB16) blindly

These stain everything they touch. Add to a pool of other color, never the reverse. Rinse brush before using other colors after PG7.

Zbukvic Framework

From Mastering Atmosphere and Mood in Watercolor by Joseph Zbukvic. Applied to this palette system.

The Watercolor Clock — Consistency Levels

Tea
also: Watery		Weakest transparent wash. Lightest tone — misty, atmospheric. Runs freely on tilted palette. Use for far distance, first washes on damp paper, gentle backgrounds.
CoffeeStrong translucent color — bright and light-filled. Quarter-tone wash. Use for background/sky washes, light shadow fill, underpainting.
Milk
also: Juicy		Half-tone — the most frequently used consistency. Moves slowly on tilted palette. Use for most midground work, architectural surfaces, foliage masses.
Cream
also: Pasty		Three-quarter tone — strongest color notes. Barely moves on tilted palette. Use for foreground darks, saturated accents, shadow passages.
ButterFull tone — no water. Sticks to palette. Reserve for the very last marks only: darkest darks, stop-lights, small figures, final accents.

Paper States & Edge Control

Dry Paper

Hard-edged shapes, dry-brush texture, drawing and scraping, architectural lines. The time for sharp effects.

Damp Paper

Broken edges, lifting pigment, scratching out soft texture.

Moist Paper

Soft controlled shapes. Best for misting effects, shaping and blending. Still directional.

Wet Paper

Soft, lost, or uncontrolled edges. Pure atmosphere. Minimal control — respond to what the paper does.

Four Pillars — Ranked by Importance

1. Drawing & Composition

The foundation. Compositional decisions made before painting determine everything else. Work out the design with concept words and notan thumbnails first.

2. Tonal Value

Value does more work than color. A painting with correct values reads clearly; one with correct color but wrong values fails. Work strictly light to dark.

3. Edges

Edges direct the eye and create mood. Hard edges advance; soft edges recede. Use paper dampness (clock) to control edge character.

4. Color

Color is the last pillar — least important, most discussed. A correct color in the wrong value does not fix a painting. Choose a Mother Color and subordinate everything to it.

Three Mood Scales

Soft & Hazy

Wet paper throughout. Tea and Coffee dominance. Lost edges, atmospheric blur, high-key values. Mist, fog, rain, soft morning light.

Medium Mellow

Mixed paper states. Coffee to Cream range. Some hard edges against soft. The majority of urban sketches. Sun in clouds, afternoon city light.

Sharp Staccato

Dry paper dominance. Milk to Butter range. Maximum contrast, hard edges everywhere. Bright midday sun, strong directional light, dramatic architectural scenes.

Field Protocol — Pre-Sketch Sequence

1. Concept Words (before drawing)

Write 3–5 words describing the feeling, not the content. Drive all subsequent decisions. Evaluate the finished sketch against these words.

2. Notan Thumbnail

2-minute value sketch — two tones only. Work out the composition. Decide what stays white before touching the paper.

3. Identify Mother Color & Mood Scale

Which of the three mood scales fits? What is the dominant color temperature? Commit before mixing anything.

4. Evaluate & Compress

What can be simplified? Ruthless selectivity — every element included is a decision. What does not serve the concept words does not belong in the painting.

Painting Sequence — Light to Dark

Always light to dark, transparent before opaque. Sky first, then distance, midground, foreground. Never revisit a passage until fully dry. Consistency sequence moves Tea → Butter as the painting advances — never in reverse.

Honest gap analysis (inventory reviewed April 2026)

Soft mop round (mid-size): No dedicated #8–#10 mop round. The Neptune quills and Tintoretto quills partially cover this role, but a true mop gives a rounder, fatter mark with different wet-into-wet behavior for organic foliage passages. Not the same tool.

Fine rigger: The Aqua Elite Rigger #8 and Series 336 Extended Point cover linework, but nothing finer. For very fine architectural detail — thin cables, delicate mullions — a #2 or #4 rigger is a materially different instrument.

Fan brush: Absent. Used by some urban sketchers for dry-brush grass and foliage texture. Not essential but notably missing.

Overall rating: 8/10 as an intermediate–advanced collection. Strong at the premium ends (Rosemary sable, Tintoretto quills, Cheap Joe's Skippers, Aqua Elite). The mid-tier rounds create uneven quality relative to the premium tools. The quill end is genuinely professional-grade — better than most working watercolorists carry in the studio. For sub-A3 urban sketching, the travel kit likely outperforms the studio kit in day-to-day use.

Working properties: Neocolor II wax pastels are water-soluble — they can be applied dry for textural marks, then dissolved with a wet brush to create wash effects. They can be layered over dried watercolor or used beneath it. The wax binder means they resist full dissolution; pigment intensity increases with more water and working time.

Key uses alongside watercolor: Texture on dry passages (dry application, not dissolved), resist effects under watercolor washes, final detail accents over dried watercolor (especially lights that can't be lifted), and toned paper work where the wax holds color on the surface rather than soaking in.

Lightfastness note: Most Neocolor II colors are lightfast (ASTM I or II), but a few brighter hues (Light Peony, Tyrian Pink, some fluorescent-influenced colors) have lower ratings. Check individual color specifications for archival work.

The Field
& the Studio
Watercolor Reference
Edition I · 2026
Ryan's Working Practice
Two parallel routines, drawn from the masters and disciplined by the Fairview Finder. The field protocol forces decision-making before the brush moves. The studio protocol exchanges urgency for depth — same skeleton, longer phases, more iteration.
How to use: Don't run both routines on the same painting. Pick one based on context. Both follow the same six-phase backbone — Observe, Notan, Draw, Wash, Accents, Assess — because the masters' framework doesn't change between bench and easel. What changes is timing, brush load, and how many iterations you allow yourself before commitment. Keep this reference within reach. Mark it up. Replace what doesn't survive contact with reality.
Routine I · On Location
The Field Protocol
42–53 minutes. One painting per session, executed honestly. Constraint forces decision.
I.Arrive & Wonder10 min · no tools
Walk before you sketch. Pause, breathe, and ask what's actually here.

Three questions, in order:

  1. What is the light doing? Where is it coming from? How long until it shifts? If less than 45 min of stable light, paint it as it is, not as it might become.
  2. What is the one thing? (Whitney's hero — Fairview's Hero prompt.) Name it before drawing. If you can't, you'll paint everything equally and lose the painting.
  3. What is the mood? Assign temperature (warm or cool dominance) and direction (horizontal / vertical / oblique). Declared before sketching.
"What's alive here?" — not what's pretty, what has tension. That's where the painting lives.
II.Notan + Significant Words5 min
Solve the design before touching good paper. Translate, don't transcribe.

Use the Fairview Finder first. Pick the window matching your panel. Test 2–3 crops. Apply Golden Ratio marks (treat as off-center placement aid — the rule of thirds works for the same reason).

Then write 3–5 significant words in the corner of the page. Concept, not inventory. "Heavy light, warm stone, cool shadow pattern" — not "buildings, trees, sky."

Then the notan. 2×3 inches, three values only: white paper, mid-gray, dark. Diluted Payne's Gray or Burnt Umber. No color. No detail.

  • Dark pattern reads as a clear, connected shape?
  • Whites concentrated, not scattered?
  • One value dominates ~60%? Other two split 40%?
  • Words and Zbukvic mood scale agree? (Soft & Hazy / Medium Mellow / Sharp Staccato)

If the thumbnail looks weak, reframe. Don't paint a bad composition hoping it'll resolve.

III.Minimal Drawing5–8 min
Map zones, not edges. Pencil disappears under paint.

2H pencil or harder. Horizon and dominant vanishing points first. Mark them even if off the page.

Reid: large shapes, not edges of things. Leave faces, foliage texture, secondary detail out — those resolve with the brush.

Do not draw shadows. Shadows are painted, not drawn.

IV.Wet Wash Commitment15–20 min
Largest to smallest. Lightest to darkest. No exceptions.

Before each major wash, say the consistency aloud — Tea, Coffee, Milk, Cream. Forces conscious decision. Stops reflexive dipping.

Wash 1 — sky & light areas (Milk to Cream). Sets temperature key. Warm dominance: Cobalt Blue Light + breath of Yellow Ochre or Quin Gold. Cool: push toward Cerulean Chromium or Phthalo Blue RS.

Wash 2 — mid-value masses (Milk). Architectural walls, distant foliage, midground. Connect shapes. Lose edges into each other while wet.

Wash 3 — shadow shapes (Cream). One coherent color, not many. Sargent & Reid: shadows in the same light source are the same family. French Ultramarine + Burnt Sienna, biased warm or cool. Mix the full puddle before you start.

Don't touch a drying wash. Horadam's gum arabic concentration means lifting attempts destroy the surface. Place it and leave it.

Look at the subject longer than the paper. 60/40 attention ratio. When anxiety creeps in, you've flipped it — that's the cue to look up.

V.Accents & Edges5–8 min
Sargent's accents. Few, deliberate, authoritative.

Butter consistency. Doorways, window depths, cast shadow edges, figures.

Count your accents. More than a dozen significant darks and each one loses authority.

Edge hierarchy. Hard edges only at the focal zone. Damp brush dragged along a dried edge dissolves it. Foreground hard → midground broken → background lost.

Figures last. Two strokes for head and hat, three for body, one for shadow under feet. Correct scale, correct value, shadow anchor. Reid: gesture over anatomy.

VI.Stop & Note2 min · brush down
Step back an arm's length. Judge against intent, not scene.
  • Does the notan still hold in the finished painting?
  • Is there one clear point of interest?
  • Did temperature dominance survive?
  • Did the painting translate the words, or transcribe the inventory?

Yes to all = the painting works regardless of execution roughness. Rough edges and blooms are honest field marks, not failures.

If no: name which decision broke (composition, value key, or temperature drift). Write it in a small note. Pattern recognition over time.

Routine II · In the Studio
The Studio Protocol
90–180 minutes. Same skeleton. Longer phases, more iteration, deeper revision.
I.Reference Selection & Wonder15–25 min
The studio risk: copying the photo instead of designing the painting.

Reference review. If working from photos, lay out 3+ references. Reject any with blown highlights or no shadow info — root cause of most studio failures is upstream reference selection.

Same three questions (light / hero / mood). The difference: in the studio you can change the light. Decide whether you're painting it as photographed or recasting it.

Significant words: 3–5 again. Studio adds a concept layer — pick a Design-for-Intent pairing. Farewell → opening diagonals, receding scale, thinning light. Awe → vast masses, tiny human notes, towering value shifts. Name the feeling, then build to it.

II.Multiple Notans & Compositional Choice15–25 min
In the field, one thumbnail. In the studio, three. Pick the strongest before drawing.

Three notans, three crops. Use the Fairview Finder to test crops on the reference. Each thumbnail commits to a different composition type.

  • Triangle → static pyramid? Tilt the apex.
  • Offset → see-saw? Adjust counterweight via value, not size.
  • S-Curve → rollercoaster? Soften the zig.
  • Tunnel → empty payoff? Put something at the end.
  • Diagonal → ejecting the eye? Block the corner exit.

Mother Color decided here. Quin Gold (warm), French Ultramarine (cool), Burnt Sienna (earthy unity), Quin Rose (dawn/storm). Trace amounts go into every wash later.

Ten-foot test. Pin the chosen notan to the wall, walk back. If it doesn't read at 10 feet in three values, simplify before you start — not after.

III.Considered Drawing15–30 min
More accuracy than the field allows. Same restraint about what gets drawn.

2H pencil. Refine perspective and proportion. Use a proportional divider or grid if the architecture demands it.

Still no shadows drawn. Still no faces, foliage texture, or secondary detail.

Pre-painting accumulation (Wood). Full puddles ready before brush touches paper: shadow mix, sky mix, mid-value mix. Mix more than you think you need.

IV.Layered Wash Build30–60 min
Wood's three-phase structure. Glaze first, structured middle values, accents last.

Glaze 1 — abstract underpainting (Tea to Coffee). Wood's method: an overall warm or cool glaze setting the temperature key. Mother Color whispers here.

Glaze 2 — structured middle values (Milk). Connect mid-value masses. Wait for full drying between layers — glaze a second mid-value pass without disturbing the first.

Glaze 3 — shadow architecture (Cream). Single coherent shadow color. Temperature gradient inside the shadow (O'Hara): warm toward the light source side, cool toward the shadow's edge.

Drying breaks are real work. Step back, assess against the notan and the words. If the painting has drifted from intent, identify what broke now — before the next layer locks it in.

"Identify what you don't like before you can fix it." — Schmid. The studio reward is having time to actually do this.
V.Accents, Edges, Atmospheric Tuning15–30 min
Same Sargent discipline. More opportunity to refine without overworking.

Butter consistency darks. Count them. Same dozen-or-fewer rule applies in the studio.

Edge audit. Reid's check: are all edges the same? Then edges are doing nothing. Hard at focal, broken at midground, lost at background.

Atmospheric anchoring (O'Hara). Distant darks lighter than close darks. Distant lights darker than close lights. Check explicitly before declaring the painting done.

The temptation here is to keep going. Set a stop rule before starting Phase V and honor it.

VI.Rest, Re-evaluate, Titleovernight + 15 min
Wendie Kause: 5 minutes, 5 feet, 5 hours, 5 days. Studio uses the longer scales.

Rest the painting overnight minimum. Titian turned paintings to the wall for months. You need separation from the heat of execution.

  • Does it work at 10 feet (impact) AND reward at 2 feet (nuance)?
  • Did I confuse "more" with "better" anywhere?
  • Does the notan still hold? One clear focal? Temperature dominance survived? Words translated?

Title the work deliberately. Symbolic, Metaphorical, Poetic, Narrative, Sensory, Time-Specific. The title extends the painting's intent.

Lesson log entry. One sentence on what you learned.

Composition Types · Design Side

Choose One Structure

16 types from Edgar Payne. Useful as a checklist; real paintings combine 2–3.

  • Balance — calm, two masses across
  • Oval — looping, eye returns
  • S-Curve — rhythmic journey
  • U-Shape — cradle, framed center
  • Triangle — stable hierarchy
  • Cross — anchored skeleton
  • Radiating — energy outward
  • Corner (C/L) — wraps the scene
  • Offset — uneven balance, very common
  • Three Beats — main / second / discovery
  • Cluster — massing, professional fast
  • Scatter — distributed rhythm
  • Diagonal — momentum, tension
  • Tunnel — depth with destination
  • Silhouette — bold readable shape
  • Pattern — repetition + a break

Skip the golden ratio mysticism — use the marks as off-center placement aid.

Design for Intent

Feeling → Form Pairings

Name the feeling first, then build the design to carry it.

  • Farewell → opening diagonals, receding scale, thinning light
  • Arrival → converging lines, closing forms, swelling light
  • Awe → vast masses, tiny human notes, towering value shifts
  • Calm → long horizontals, wide spacing, softened edges
  • Menace → oblique horizons, pressurized darks, tight edges
  • Intimacy → overlapping shapes, compressed space
  • Authority → erect rhythms, economy of accents
  • Mystery → simplify shapes, subdue contrasts, suggest
  • Joy → bright notes climbing, sparkling accents
  • Reflection → verticals above, mirrors below

Zbukvic's Four Pillars · Ranked

When stuck, work upstream. Drawing fixes don't solve color problems; color fixes don't solve drawing problems.

Pillar 1

Drawing & Composition

The bones. If broken, nothing downstream can rescue the painting. Field check: notan. Studio check: 10-foot test pinned to wall.

Pillar 2

Tonal Value

"90% of the time something is wrong, it's a value problem." — Seiler. Three values minimum, dominant covering ~60%. Squint to check.

Pillar 3

Edges

"The key to masterful and ordinary paintings is the edges." — Schmid. Hard at focal only. Broken at midground. Lost at background.

Pillar 4

Color

Last in the hierarchy. "You can commit any sin with color, as long as that color is the right value." — Casey. Mother Color before first wash.

When the Painting Drifts

Diagnostic prompts. Pick one based on what's actually broken.

If scattered or lifeless

First Principles

Strip down to story, big value masses, edge control. If the 10-foot read is weak, don't add detail — regroup values, simplify shapes, place contrast on purpose.

Before any big change

Second-Order Thinking

Every "improvement" steals attention from somewhere else. If pushing this contrast, what must get quieter? Test small before committing.

If repeating the same frustration

Root Cause

Name the problem in 5 words. When did it first drift? What broke first — value, draw, edges, plan? Most problems start upstream: weak reference, weak thumbnail, unclear light logic.

Mid-painting, no momentum

OODA Loop

Observe the facts. Orient on priority. Decide the single best next move. Act cleanly. Step back. Loop.

The Working Rules

From the masters. The ones worth tattooing on the inside of your wrist.

Translate, don't transcribe.

"It's a poem, not a police report." — Genn. The scene is the prompt, not the assignment.

Refuse to hurry.

Whitney. Pre-painting decisions are where the painting is won or lost. Speed comes from clarity, not from rushing the brush.

Edit, edit, edit.

The worst reason to include something is because it's there. — Swiatoviak.

Start big. Stay big.

Stay loose, stay lost, stay abstract as long as you can. — Bermingham / Winspear. Detail too early kills depth.

Connect the darks.

Schmid. A connected dark mass reads stronger than fragmented patches. Same for the lights.

Place it. Leave it.

Don't fix things. Put it down, then put something else down. — Eugene Hall. Touching drying watercolor destroys it.

Look at the subject longer than the paper.

60/40. When that ratio flips, anxiety has taken over. Look up. Reconnect with the words.

Stop sooner than you want to.

Get in, say it, get out. — Herron. "It takes five people to paint a painting: one to paint, four to make them stop."
